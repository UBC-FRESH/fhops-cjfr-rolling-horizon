\section*{Abstract}
Rolling-horizon optimization is widely used to schedule forest harvesting machines, but the planning
horizon and re-optimization interval are chosen with little evidence. We evaluated both with the
open-source Forest Harvesting Operations Planning System
(FHOPS) on nine semi-synthetic British Columbia case studies (three timber supply areas, 6--40
blocks, 6--24 machines, 112-day horizon), crossing four planning horizons (2--16 weeks), three
re-optimization intervals (1, 7, 14 days), and two methods (simulated annealing and mixed-integer
programming). Each window started from the state left by the previous plan (remaining volume,
staged inventory, machine positions), and every run was scored by replaying its stitched schedule
against the full problem. Almost all configurations delivered close to the best plan found for their
scenario, though \NRunsBeyondFourPct\ of \NRollingRuns\ runs fell more than 4\% short (worst
\RelBestWorstRolling\%). Horizon length beyond about four weeks gained little and the longest horizon
was no better, though that comparison is confounded with solver tractability; daily and weekly
re-optimization were indistinguishable, and only a 14-day commitment period carried a small penalty.
Failed windows were a solver effect, but the weakest simulated-annealing runs had none, so
configuration mattered too. An evaluation that scores single windows without carrying state can
misstate plan quality, as an earlier evaluation did.

\vspace{1em}
\noindent\textbf{Key words:} forest operations planning; machine scheduling; rolling horizon;
mixed-integer programming; simulated annealing; open-source decision support
