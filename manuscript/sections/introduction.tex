\section{Introduction}

Forest management decisions are made on a hierarchy of planning levels. Strategic plans set
sustainable harvest levels and silvicultural pathways over decades, tactical plans allocate
blocks, roads, and contractors over one to several years, and operational plans decide what is
harvested, with which machines, and when, over days to weeks
\citep{martell1998forest,bouchard2017integrated}. Operations research (OR) has supported all three
levels for half a century \citep{ronnqvist2023fifty}, but the operational level remains the least
served. Its problems are detailed, site-specific, and combinatorial, and they are revised
continually as conditions change \citep{epstein2007harvest,ronnqvist2015operations,
santos2019mathematical}. The stakes are nevertheless high. Delays in deploying equipment disrupt
wood supply to mills and raise operating costs \citep{d2008using,alayet2013planning}, and
operational decisions determine the volume, timing, and quality of the wood delivered
\citep{shahi2018integrating}. These effects are amplified where weather, machine breakdowns, and
markets make conditions uncertain \citep{arora2023detailed}.

Machine scheduling is a core operational problem. It assigns machines to harvest blocks over
discrete time periods, subject to machine availability, landing capacity, and the precedence
between harvesting phases \citep{corner2005scheduling}. In a ground-based system, for example,
felled stems must be skidded before they are processed, and processed before they are loaded, so
each phase depends on the inventory staged by the phase before it. Practice ranges from
spreadsheets, whiteboards, and experience in small and medium operations to proprietary
optimization software and consultant-built tools in large ones. In a systematic review of 23
OR-based forest operational planning models, none provided fully open-source artefacts, only one shared its data, and none reported explicit validation \citep{jaffray2026systematic,viana2023modelling}.
Design choices in planning systems are therefore rarely evaluated in a way that others can
reproduce, and findings about how to configure such systems are hard to transfer between
operations.

Deterministic optimization over a full season is often intractable for machine scheduling
\citep{bellavenutte2020partitioning}. Long deterministic schedules also lose value as conditions
change \citep{sethi1991theory}. Re-planning at regular intervals is the time-tested response of
forest planners to this uncertainty, with or without mathematical models
\citep{martell1998forest,ulvdal2022uncertainty}. Rolling-horizon planning formalizes that
practice. It solves a sequence of shorter subproblems, implements (locks) the first part of each
solution, advances time, and re-optimizes from the new state \citep{baker1979analytic,
venkataraman1999effect}. Near-term decisions are thus fixed while later decisions stay flexible,
which is also the logic of hierarchical planning with anticipation
\citep{beaudoin2008hierarchical,feng2010simulation}. Despite this, rolling-horizon methods are
rarely examined in forest operations research. Only one of the 23 operational models in the review
above used a rolling horizon \citep{arora2023detailed,jaffray2026thesis}.

Rolling-horizon performance depends on two design parameters: the length of each planning window
(the planning horizon) and the interval between re-optimizations, which is also the span of
decisions committed at each step. Operations research theory suggests a forecast horizon beyond
which further look-ahead does not change the first decisions, and therefore adds little
\citep{baker1979analytic,sethi1991theory,chand2002forecast}. Studies in manufacturing and supply
chain planning show that short re-planning intervals can improve responsiveness. They can also
make schedules nervous, because frequent revisions disrupt those who execute them
\citep{venkataraman1999effect,sahin2008master,herding2024rolling}. Long horizons, in turn, raise
the size of every subproblem and the time needed to solve it \citep{santos2021integrated}. In
practice, horizons and re-planning cycles are often set by convention, contractor coordination,
or business cycles rather than by evidence. In forest machine scheduling we found no systematic
evaluation of how these two parameters trade solution quality against schedule stability and
computing effort. Uncertainty is usually the motivation for re-planning, but the structural
effect of the two parameters can be isolated under deterministic conditions first. Such a
deterministic baseline is needed before uncertainty can be added, because it separates what
decomposition costs from what re-planning gains.

A sound evaluation also depends on how the rolling procedure is implemented and how its output is
scored. Each window must start from the state left by the decisions already implemented. In a
multi-phase harvesting system that state includes the volume remaining in each block, the
inventory staged between phases, and the position of each machine. A rolling plan should be
judged by the schedule that is actually implemented, that is, the stitched sequence of locked
decisions, evaluated against the full problem. The objective of any single window is not a
measure of that schedule. An implementation that omits the carried state, or a metric based on a
single window, can produce horizon and frequency effects that are artifacts. Solver behaviour is
a further confounder. When exact solvers are given a fixed time per window, large windows can
end without a useful solution, and this can be mistaken for an effect of the horizon itself.
\citet{jaffray2026thesis} reported a first evaluation of these parameters with an early release
of the open-source Forest Harvesting Operations Planning System (FHOPS). That release carried no
state between windows, and the evaluation scored each run by its last window's objective. Those
two choices make the reported numbers a poor measure of the schedule that is actually implemented,
and we treat them as superseded rather than reproducible. FHOPS~1.0.1 \citep{fhops2026v101}
carries the operational state forward and replays the stitched plan against the full problem.

This study repeats that evaluation with FHOPS~1.0.1 (\fhopspaper) on nine semi-synthetic case
studies representative of British Columbia (BC) harvesting operations, with both an exact and a
heuristic solution method. Its contributions are threefold. First, it provides evidence on how
planning horizon and re-optimization interval affect the delivered volume, feasibility, and
continuity of implemented machine schedules, and the computing effort needed to produce them.
Second, it sets out an evaluation protocol for rolling-horizon planning systems: carried state,
stitched-plan replay, explicit reporting of window outcomes, and calibrated solver limits. The
protocol is stated in terms of the operational state and solver behaviour that any such system
shares, so it transfers beyond FHOPS, although we demonstrate it only on FHOPS here. Third, it releases every scenario,
script, and run output, so that the results can be reproduced and extended. We ask:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item At what planning horizon length do further gains in delivered volume become small?
  \item Does frequent re-optimization outperform longer planning windows that are updated less
        often?
  \item How do horizon length and re-optimization frequency affect schedule continuity and the
        feasibility of the implemented schedule?
  \item How do these effects depend on problem size and on the solution method (exact
        mixed-integer programming versus simulated annealing), including the computing time each
        configuration requires?
\end{enumerate}
