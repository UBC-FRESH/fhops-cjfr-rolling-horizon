\section{Results}

We analysed \NRollingRuns\ rolling-horizon runs and \NBaselineRuns\ full-horizon baselines
(\NRuns\ runs in total; Table~\ref{tab:scenarios} describes the scenarios). Every run is scored on
its stitched plan, replayed against the full 112-day problem. Before the grid, a calibration fixed
the per-window time limits and the MIP solver; Section~\ref{sec:calibration} reports it.

\subsection{Solver calibration and solver choice}
\label{sec:calibration}

The per-window limits and the MIP solver were fixed before the grid, from a calibration on the first
window of eight representative configurations (Section~\ref{sec:solution-methods}). Each calibration
window was solved with a 7200-s first stage and an 1800-s second stage, recording the incumbent and
bound over time. At 1800~s the first-stage incumbent was within 0.4\% of its 7200-s value in six of
the seven windows not solved to optimality, and within 1.1\% in the largest (40 blocks, 112 days).
The remaining optimality gaps (0.4--5.2\% at 1800~s) narrowed little by 7200~s, and no first-stage
incumbent is recorded before 1800~s in the largest window's trajectory checkpoints, so shorter limits
were not adequate there. The second stage converged more slowly: at 300~s its objective was up to
23\% below its 1800-s value, and at 900~s within 3\% in all but one window, where it stayed well
below (the 40-block, 14-day Prince George calibration; Supplementary Table~S1).

The solver comparison used six windows (sizes 18 and 40) with an 1800-s first stage and a 300-s
second stage. HiGHS~1.15.1 returned only the empty plan on three of them; Gurobi returned non-empty
plans on all six (Supplementary Table~S2).

\subsection{Tractability and window outcomes}

The full-horizon baselines set the reference for each scenario (Table~\ref{tab:baselines}). They
are not uniformly good. For Kamloops and Prince George at 6 and 18 blocks the baseline reaches
100\% of the scheduled volume. Elsewhere it delivers less---\BaselineShareNILow--\BaselineShareNIHigh\%
for North Island at every size, and \BaselineShareLargeLow--\BaselineShareLargeHigh\% for the
40-block Kamloops and Prince George cases---because of fleet throughput, landing capacity, or
solver limits, which this design does not separate.

Solver behaviour explains much of this (Table~\ref{tab:windows}). The MIP reached its 1800~s
first-stage limit in \WindowsStageOneLimitMIPSix\% of windows at 6 blocks,
\WindowsStageOneLimitMIPEighteen\% at 18 blocks, and \WindowsStageOneLimitMIPForty\% at 40 blocks.
The simulated annealing never reached a limit, because it stops after a fixed number of iterations.
Across all MIP rolling windows, \NWindowsNoSolution\ returned no solution and \NWindowsEmpty\ were empty, all at 18 and 40 blocks;
no simulated-annealing window failed. All \GurobiSolvesLogged\ MIP solves longer than 100~s ran at a
median of \GurobiWorkRateMedian\ work units per second (5th--95th percentile
\GurobiWorkRateLow--\GurobiWorkRateHigh), about half the rate measured on an otherwise idle host.

\subsection{Delivered volume: planning horizon and re-optimization interval}

Delivered volume is close to the best plan found for each scenario in most configurations
(Fig.~\ref{fig:delivered}). Against the same-method full-horizon baseline ($R^{\mathrm{base}}$,
Eq.~\ref{eq:relative}) the median rolling run reaches \RelBaselineMedianMIP\% (MIP) and
\RelBaselineMedianSA\% (SA), so decomposing the horizon costs little at the median; the worst run
reaches \RelBaselineMin\%. Aggregated over the three timber supply areas and all sizes, horizons and lock spans, the median run delivers
\RelBestMedianMIPLockOne\% (MIP) and \RelBestMedianSALockOne\% (SA) of the best known plan at a
1-day lock; \RelBestMedianMIPLockSeven\% and \RelBestMedianSALockSeven\% at 7 days; and
\RelBestMedianMIPLockFourteen\% and \RelBestMedianSALockFourteen\% at 14 days. The worst runs reach
\RelBestMinMIPLockOne\% (MIP) and \RelBestMinSALockOne\% (SA) at a 1-day lock, and
\RelBestMinMIPLockFourteen\% and \RelBestMinSALockFourteen\% at 14 days.

The differences between configurations are small. At 40 blocks, where the effects are largest, the
median relative volume by window length and lock span is:

Two patterns stand out. First, window length matters little: the 4- and 8-week windows are the best
or tied-best in every lock span, and the 16-week windows are no better and are sometimes a little
worse. The 16-week penalty coincides with the point at which MIP windows most often hit the time
limit, so it is at least partly a tractability effect rather than a planning one. Second, the
re-optimization interval matters only at its longest value: the 1-day and 7-day locks are
indistinguishable, and the 14-day lock is 1--3 percentage points lower at 40 blocks.

\subsection{Feasibility and day-to-day machine continuity}

Feasibility of the replayed plans differs by method. All \NRollingRunsSA\ simulated-annealing plans
replay with no sequencing violations. \NRunsWithViolationsMIPRolling\ of the \NRollingRunsMIP\ MIP
rolling runs show raw violations at the FHOPS default tolerance of $10^{-6}$~m$^3$
(\NViolationsMIPRolling\ in total). The count falls steeply as the tolerance rises through the
sub-litre range: \NRunsWithMaterialViolationsOneEMinusFour\ runs at $10^{-4}$~m$^3$,
\NRunsWithMaterialViolations\ at $10^{-3}$~m$^3$, and
\NRunsWithMaterialViolationsOneEMinusTwo\ at $10^{-2}$~m$^3$. That fall-off, together with
\NTinyRowsTotal\ MIP assignment rows whose planned production is below $10^{-3}$~m$^3$ (up to
\NTinyRowsMax\ in one run), identifies the raw violations as numerical artefacts of
negligible-production rows rather than operational infeasibilities (UBC-FRESH/fhops\#157). Feasibility is therefore not a discriminating factor between
configurations; it is a property of the solver.

Day-to-day machine continuity is descriptive here, because machine moves are free in these
scenarios and neither method is rewarded for keeping a machine on a block. Median continuity is high
at 6 blocks (\ContinuityMedianSASix\ for SA, \ContinuityMedianMIPSix\ for MIP) and falls with size,
more so for MIP (\ContinuityMedianMIPEighteen\ at 18 blocks, \ContinuityMedianMIPForty\ at 40) than
for SA (\ContinuityMedianSAEighteen\ and \ContinuityMedianSAForty). Machine moves rise with size and
are slightly higher for MIP at 40 blocks. These are incidental properties of the schedules, not optimized ones.

\subsection{Problem size, solution method, and computing effort}

Problem size has the clearest effect (Fig.~\ref{fig:effort}). At 6 blocks the median run reaches the
best known plan for both methods, though not every North Island run does. At 18 blocks the median
MIP run reaches \RelBestMedianMIPEighteen\% and the median SA run \RelBestMedianSAEighteen\%; at 40
blocks they reach \RelBestMedianMIPForty\% and \RelBestMedianSAForty\%.

Measured against the MIP run of the same configuration, SA delivers more volume in
\NPairsSAWinsAny\ of \NPairsAll\ pairs (by more than 0.5\% in \NPairsSAWins\ of them), reaching up to
1.11 times the MIP volume. The remaining pairs are MIP's, but the gap is not always a solver
failure: in \NPairsSAWinsMIPClean\ of the \NPairsSAWinsAny\ pairs where SA won, the MIP returned a
complete plan in every window. The MIP advantage comes at a cost: median wall time at 40 blocks is
\WallMedianHoursMIPForty\ h for MIP against \WallMedianHoursSAForty\ h for SA, and at 18 blocks
\WallMedianHoursMIPEighteen\ h against \WallMedianHoursSAEighteen\ h. The weakest MIP cells are the
ones with no-solution or empty windows.
