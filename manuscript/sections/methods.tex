\section{Materials and methods}

\subsection{Case studies}

We used nine semi-synthetic scenarios: three timber supply areas (North Island, Kamloops, and Prince
George timber supply areas, BC) crossed with three problem sizes (6, 18, and 40 harvest blocks;
Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}) \citep{jaffray2026zenodo}. Block geometry, area, and stand attributes
come from an overlay of the provincial Forest Harvest Authorizations and Vegetation Resources
Inventory layers \citep{bcgov_fha,bcgov_vri}. Average ground slope per block was derived from a
digital elevation model. Every block is harvested with the same ground-based system: feller-buncher,
grapple skidder, processor, then loader. Machine productivity (m$^3$\,day$^{-1}$, per machine and
block) was predicted from stand and terrain attributes with the productivity models bundled with
FHOPS: feller-bunchers after \citet{lahrsen2025thesis}, grapple skidders after \citet{kosicki2005adv6n7},
processors after \citet{berry2019processor}, and live-heel loaders after \citet{harvey1982tn46},
with a floor of
1~m$^3$\,day$^{-1}$. Fleets of 6, 12, and 24 machines were split as evenly as possible across the
four roles. Each block has its own landing, which at most two machines can work at once. The
planning horizon is 112 days with three 8-h shifts per day, 7 days per week, and full machine
availability. Blocks can be harvested at any time within the horizon. The scenarios differ in
total volume relative to the fleet, and in some of them the baseline runs do not deliver the full
scheduled volume within 112 days (Table~\ref{tab:scenarios}); the design cannot separate a
fleet-throughput limit from model, landing, or solver limits. Delivered volume, rather than
completion time, is therefore the primary measure of plan quality.

\subsection{Scheduling model and solution methods}

FHOPS represents each scenario as a shift-level machine scheduling problem: which machine works on
which block in each shift. The objective maximizes delivered volume, subject to machine
availability, landing capacity, and the precedence between harvesting phases. A downstream phase
can only process volume that the upstream phase has already staged at the landing. The
mixed-integer linear programming (MIP) formulation is documented with the software
\citep{fhops2026formulation} and in \citet{paradis2026fhops}; we do not restate it here. The objective rewards
volume delivered by the final phase and penalises volume left undelivered, both with weight 1. The
scenarios define no inter-block distances, so the mobilisation term is zero and machines move
between blocks at no cost. Landing capacity is a hard constraint.

Each window was solved with one of two methods. (i) Simulated annealing (SA), the FHOPS
metaheuristic: 500 iterations per window, random seed 42. (ii) The FHOPS operational MIP, built
with Pyomo \citep{bynum2021pyomo} and solved with \mipbackend\ \citep{gurobi2026manual} from a cold start (no heuristic warm start) on one thread, with a limit of
\miptimelimit\ per window. In rolling windows FHOPS also runs a lexicographic second stage. It
favours earlier production without worsening the first-stage objective, so that a window does not
postpone work beyond its locked span \citep{fhops2026v101}. This stage was limited to
\earlinesslimit.

The limits were set from a calibration on the first window of eight representative configurations
(sizes 6--40, windows of 14--112 days). Each was solved with a 7200-s first stage and an 1800-s
second stage, and the incumbent and bound were recorded over time. At 1800~s the first-stage
incumbent was within 0.4\% of its 7200-s value in six of the seven windows not solved to
optimality, and within 1.1\% in the largest (40 blocks, 112 days). The remaining optimality gaps
(0.4--5.2\% at 1800~s) narrowed little by 7200~s. Shorter limits were not adequate for the largest
window: no first-stage incumbent is recorded before 1800~s in its trajectory checkpoints. The second
stage converged more slowly. At 300~s its objective was up to 23\% below its 1800-s value, and at
900~s within 3\% in all but one window, where it stayed well below (the 40-block, 14-day Prince
George calibration; Supplementary Table~S1). We used Gurobi for the MIP arm because of a comparison with the
open-source solver HiGHS~1.15.1 \citep{huangfu2018highs} on six windows (sizes 18 and 40), with an
1800-s first stage and a 300-s second stage. HiGHS returned only the empty plan on three of them;
Gurobi returned non-empty plans on all six. Supplementary Tables S1 and S2 give the calibration and the solver comparison.\footnote{Supplementary material is available with the article through the journal Web site.}

\subsection{Rolling-horizon procedure with state carry-forward}

For a master horizon of $H = 112$ days, a planning window of $\theta$ days, and a re-optimization
interval (lock span) of $f \le \theta$ days, FHOPS~1.0.1 proceeds as follows (Fig.~\ref{fig:rolling};
\citealp{fhops2026v101}):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Solve the subproblem for days $[t, \min(t+\theta-1, H)]$, starting at $t=1$.
  \item Lock the decisions for days $[t, t+f-1]$ and append them to the stitched plan.
  \item Replay the stitched plan with the deterministic playback engine to obtain the carried
        state: the volume remaining in each block, the remaining output, staged inventory, and
        shift counts of each phase, and the last block of each machine.
  \item Set $t \leftarrow t+f$ and repeat from that state until $t > H$.
\end{enumerate}
A window for which the solver returns no solution locks nothing for its span and is recorded as
such. A window with nothing left to plan is recorded as skipped. A window that returns a plan
delivering no volume is recorded as empty. Earlier FHOPS releases reset every window to the
initial scenario state, and \citet{jaffray2026thesis} used one of them. Re-planning the full block
volume in every window, with staged inventory reset, inflated the apparent effect of the
re-optimization interval. The results reported here supersede those values.

\subsection{Experimental design}

The full factorial design crossed the nine scenarios with
$\theta \in \{2, 4, 8, 16\}$~weeks, $f \in \{1, 7, 14\}$~days, and both solution methods, giving
\NRollingRuns\ rolling-horizon runs, plus one full-horizon baseline per scenario and method
($\theta = f = H$), for \NBaselineRuns\ baselines (\NRuns\ runs in total). These levels represent
daily, weekly, and biweekly re-planning cycles and horizons from two weeks to the full season.
A rolling run solves $\lceil H/f \rceil$ windows: 112, 16, or 8 for $f = 1$, 7, and 14 days. Window
lengths of 2--16 weeks span a wide range of problem sizes. For the MIP, the window models range from
about 10\,000 binary variables (18 blocks, 14 days) to about 350\,000 (40 blocks, 112 days)
(Table~S1). Inputs were identical across
windows, so differences between runs come only from the rolling-horizon configuration. Runs were
executed in parallel, one single-threaded process per run, on a Linux server with 36 physical
cores (72 logical) and 754~GB of memory. The MIP runs were executed first, 36 at a time (one per physical
core) with no other load, so that each time-limited solve had a full core. The SA runs stop after a
fixed number of iterations, so their results do not depend on machine load; they were run
afterwards. With 36 concurrent solves, Gurobi
ran at a median of \GurobiWorkRateMedian\ work units\,s$^{-1}$ (5th--95th percentile
\GurobiWorkRateLow--\GurobiWorkRateHigh; \GurobiSolvesLogged\ solves longer than 100~s), against
0.9--1.2 in the calibration, which ran eight solves at a time. Each grid window therefore received
somewhat less solver effort than the same time limit gave in the calibration. The software stack was FHOPS~1.0.1,
Pyomo~6.10.1, Python~3.12.3, and gurobipy~13.0.3.

Generative AI coding agents assisted with the experiment and analysis code under the direction of
G.P. Kimi K3 (Moonshot AI) assisted with the original version of the rolling-horizon runner.
Claude Opus 5.5 (Anthropic, used through OpenCode) assisted with the solver probe and time-limit
calibration scripts, extensions to the runner (solver selection, worker pools, solver logging),
and the scripts that compute the performance measures and generate all figures and tables. The authors reviewed the code, checked
its outputs against the raw run files, and take responsibility for the results. All scripts are
released with the data (see Data availability). The design, implementation, and testing of the FHOPS package were assisted by OpenAI GPT-5.x
models (several versions over the development period).

\subsection{Performance measures}

Each run was scored on its stitched plan, replayed against the full 112-day scenario with FHOPS
deterministic playback. We report:
\begin{itemize}
  \item delivered volume (m$^3$ output by the final phase);
  \item relative delivered volume against two references. For run $r$ on scenario $k$ with
        method $m$ and delivered volume $V_r$,
        \begin{equation}
          R^{\mathrm{base}}_r = \frac{V_r}{V^{\mathrm{base}}_{k,m}}, \qquad
          R^{\mathrm{best}}_r = \frac{V_r}{\max_{r' \in \mathcal{R}_k} V_{r'}},
          \label{eq:relative}
        \end{equation}
        where $V^{\mathrm{base}}_{k,m}$ is the delivered volume of the same-method full-horizon
        baseline and $\mathcal{R}_k$ is the set of all runs on scenario $k$ (both methods,
        baselines included). $R^{\mathrm{base}}$ measures the cost of decomposition for a given
        method. $R^{\mathrm{best}}$ allows comparison across methods and is not inflated when a
        baseline is itself limited by solver time;
  \item sequencing violations in the replayed plan, that is, slots in which a phase was scheduled
        to process more volume than its upstream phases had staged. We report the count at four
        volume tolerances ($10^{-6}$, $10^{-4}$, $10^{-3}$ and $10^{-2}$~m$^3$) rather than at one,
        because MIP assignments contain negligible-production rows whose replay produces spurious
        violations and the count would otherwise hinge on an arbitrary threshold; a sub-litre
        shortfall is orders of magnitude below any operational resolution;
  \item block-to-block machine moves and schedule continuity, computed from the playback records
        that produced volume. Continuity is the share of the machines working a block on one
        working day that also work it on the block's next working day, pooled over blocks and days
        \citep[adapted from][]{vanhoucke2006work}:
        \begin{equation}
          C = \frac{\sum_{b}\sum_{i} \lvert M_{b,d_i} \cap M_{b,d_{i+1}} \rvert}
                   {\sum_{b}\sum_{i} \lvert M_{b,d_i} \rvert},
          \label{eq:continuity}
        \end{equation}
        where $d_1 < d_2 < \dots$ are the days on which block $b$ is worked and $M_{b,d}$ is the
        set of machines that produced volume on block $b$ on day $d$. Moves are free in these scenarios, so neither
        method is rewarded for reducing them, and both measures describe incidental schedule
        structure rather than an optimized property;
  \item computing effort: total wall time over all windows, and the number of windows that
        reached a time limit, returned no solution, or were empty.
\end{itemize}
The design is deterministic, with one run per configuration, so we compare configurations
descriptively rather than with inferential tests. All figures, tables, and numbers in the results
are generated by scripts from the committed run outputs (see Data availability).
